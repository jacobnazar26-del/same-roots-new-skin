Why Chinatown, Why Hong Kong

緣起

In October, I traveled to Hong Kong, Shenzhen, and Guangzhou. A week later, I walked through Manhattan's Chinatown with my camera and a notebook. I expected Chinatown to feel like a smaller copy of what I had just seen. Instead, I found something more interesting.

Before I Looked Closely

I moved to New York from Los Angeles this fall to start at NYU, so Chinatown was a neighborhood I had passed through but never really seen. I knew it as a place for cheap dumplings, crowded sidewalks, and souvenir shops on Canal Street. I knew Chinese immigrants had built it, but I didn't know who they were, where they came from, or how the neighborhood had changed. Visiting Hong Kong and China right before this project gave me something most visitors don't have: a picture of the "original" to hold up against what I found on Mott Street.

Chinatown is not a copy of Hong Kong or China. It is a hybrid. The fruit stalls, roast ducks, and red lanterns are the same ones I saw across the Pacific, but here they sit under fire escapes, beside GEICO billboards, and behind boxes tagged with graffiti. Chinatown shows what happens when a culture is carried across the world: its traditions survive, but they take on the face of the city that holds them.

What I Learned

考據

(Superscript numbers open the Sources tab.)

  • Chinese immigrants began settling on Mott, Pell, and Doyers Streets in the 1870s. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 kept Chinatown a mostly male "bachelor society" for decades, until the Immigration Act of 1965 allowed families to come.1
  • Early Chinatown spoke mainly Taishanese. After 1965, Cantonese speakers from Hong Kong and Guangdong arrived. In the 1980s and '90s, immigrants from Fuzhou settled along East Broadway, and today Mandarin is increasingly common.1
  • The Chinese Consolidated Benevolent Association, founded in 1883, still serves as an umbrella group for about sixty community organizations.2
  • The Hip Sing Association on Pell Street started as a tong, a mutual-protection group for Chinese immigrants facing prejudice, and was involved in the violent "Tong Wars" of the early 1900s.3
  • The Mahayana Buddhist Temple at 133 Canal Street opened in 1997 inside a former movie theater and holds a 16-foot Buddha, the tallest in New York City.4
  • Hop Kee has served Cantonese food from its basement at 21 Mott Street since 1968.5 Great N.Y. Noodletown has been on the corner of Bowery and Bayard since 1981.6
  • Lens fact: Hong Kong's Lee Tung Avenue, where I photographed the lanterns, used to be "Wedding Card Street," famous for its print shops. It was demolished in 2007 and rebuilt as a luxury shopping street, and the original shop owners never returned.7 Every Mid-Autumn Festival, more than 800 red lanterns now hang over it.8 Gentrification is reshaping both neighborhoods.

PLACES

1

Pell Street

HONG KONG / CHINA
NEW YORK

Looking down Pell Street, the block felt narrow and tall at the same time. Red brick walk-ups rose five and six stories on both sides, their black iron fire escapes zigzagging down the fronts like stitches. Strings of round paper lanterns, gold, orange, and pale green, swung on wires stretched across the street, bumping lightly in the breeze. On the right, a long green sign with gold characters read 美洲協勝公會, the Hip Sing Association, and just below it hung a bright orange Knicks banner. A blue salon sign listed "Lee Lee Beauty & Hair Salon" in English and Chinese, with a phone number and three yellow arrows. Pigeons pecked at the asphalt, a white van idled at the curb, and above everything was a clear blue October sky with a single bird gliding over the rooftops. It smelled faintly of cooking oil and exhaust. The street was Chinese in its signs and New York in its bones.

Through the lens

In Hong Kong, the vertical signs stack just as high, but a red double-decker bus rolls beneath them. That bus is a leftover from British rule, a reminder that Hong Kong is a hybrid too.

2

Mahayana Buddhist Temple

HONG KONG / CHINA
NEW YORK

Standing across Canal Street, I could hear the temple before I could focus on it: car horns, a city bus braking, someone shouting into a phone. Then I looked up. The front of the building was pale yellow brick, and the entrance was framed by a gate of bright red pillars topped with a green tiled roof that curled up at the corners, the way roofs do in old Chinese paintings. Two small stone lions guarded the steps. Red characters climbed down the center of the wall, 大乘寺, and above them, in English, "MAHAYANA BUDDHIST TEMPLE." But directly above the temple, taking up half the sky, was a giant blue GEICO billboard with a cartoon gecko driving a red convertible, with graffiti tagged along the wall beneath it. A sacred place built in a former movie theater, sitting under an insurance ad: it was the clearest picture of Chinatown's double life I found all day.

Through the lens

In Hong Kong, an old lavender apartment block stands in front of a glass tower that reflects the clouds. Both cities show the old and the new pressed side by side.

3

The Stairs Into Hop Kee

HONG KONG / CHINA
NEW YORK

To eat at Hop Kee, you have to go down. The entrance is a steep staircase cut into the sidewalk on Mott Street, lined with polished pinkish granite that felt cool and smooth under my hand. Two metal railings run down either side, and the top edges are lined with jagged metal strips. At the bottom, through a pair of glass doors, I could see a red carpet and two signs: "HOLD THE HANDRAIL PLEASE" and a yellow "CAUTION: WATCH YOUR STEP" sign. Taped below them was a small blue wheelchair symbol. After reading Johnna Keller's "The Politics of Stairs," that symbol stopped me. The restaurant has been in this basement since 1968, and for someone in a wheelchair, or an elderly person with bad knees, those steep steps decide whether they get to eat here at all.

Through the lens

In Hong Kong, the city climbs mountains, and worn concrete stairs with peeling railings lead into shops. In both places, the stairs decide who gets in.

THINGS

4

The Noodletown Window

HONG KONG / CHINA
NEW YORK

Behind the glass at Great N.Y. Noodletown, a row of roast ducks hung from steel hooks, their skin a deep glossy amber, crackled and shining under the window lights. Beside them hung a long slab of roast pork, and a handwritten yellow sign in Chinese, 全體乳豬, advertised a whole roast suckling pig for $198. Above the window, a bright yellow sign read "Great N.Y. Noodletown" in green English letters next to red Chinese characters. In front of the window stood an older woman in a maroon puffer jacket, a pink tote bag on her arm, writing slowly on a small pad of paper with a pencil, completely ignoring the tourists taking photos around her. The smell of roasted fat and soy sauce drifted out every time the door opened. To me, that woman, with her pencil and paper, was the most Chinatown thing on the block.

Through the lens

In China, cured ducks pressed flat and red lap cheong sausages hang in a glass case the same way. The food traveled across the ocean unchanged. The sign above it did not.

5

The Graffiti Fruit Stand

HONG KONG / CHINA
NEW YORK

Under two red umbrellas sagging from the morning, a wooden fruit stall sat on the corner, and at first it looked exactly like the ones I had walked past in Hong Kong. Pale brown longans, small and round like marbles, were piled in loose clusters, still attached to their thin stems. Guavas wrapped in white foam netting sat beside dark avocados and plastic boxes of blueberries. The price signs were handwritten in thick black marker, "6.00 BOX," "5 FOR 5.00," and one had Chinese characters, 柿饼 (dried persimmon), written above the price. A young vendor in a camouflage jacket and baseball cap stood behind the counter, checking his phone between customers. But when I looked down, the front of the stall was covered in graffiti: huge looping black and white letters spray-painted across the splintered wood, the paint flaking where hands and shoulders had brushed against it. In Hong Kong, the fruit stall glowed under pink lamps. Here, it wore New York's signature. That was the moment I understood Chinatown. It isn't Hong Kong moved across the ocean. It's Hong Kong's traditions living inside New York's skin.

Through the lens

In Hong Kong, the same fruit sits on neat trays under handwritten signs reading "20元/份" (20 HK dollars per portion). Same fruit, same handwriting, different city.

6

The Bayard Street Gift Shop

HONG KONG / CHINA
NEW YORK

The gift shop on Bayard Street was so red it almost hurt to look at. Big paper lanterns with yellow tassels hung from the awning, and behind them the window was covered with red and gold banners: 生意興隆 ("prosperous business") and 大吉大利 ("great luck and great profit"). Two cartoon red horses, for the Year of the Horse, grinned from the glass door. Folding fans in pink, black, and lavender were clipped open on a spinning rack, and a folding table out front was crowded with lucky cats waving their paws, red string bracelets, and bundles of chopsticks marked "$2." Taped right in the middle of all that luck were two printed signs in English: "NOTICE: NO PHOTOS OR VIDEO ALLOWED." A store built on tradition and good fortune, run by New York rules. It was a small detail, but it's the kind of thing you'd never see on a website.

Through the lens

On Lee Tung Avenue in Hong Kong, more than 800 red lanterns hang above the street for the Mid-Autumn Festival. In Chinatown, the same lanterns hang above a folding table on Bayard Street.

5

Voices

人聲

I interviewed two people: one who lives in China and works in Hong Kong, and one who has run a restaurant in Chinatown for decades.

Nick · Lives in mainland China, works in Hong Kong, stayed in Chinatown in 2019

Nick explained that China "lost" Hong Kong to Britain and got it back in 1997. He said Hong Kong and mainland China share "the same hair, same face, same people" but have a different culture. He used the old Chinese saying that all Chinese people are "descendants of the dragon." In the past, many people wanted to move to Hong Kong because it was rich and paid good salaries. He believes that image was glamorized, and that today people are happier in mainland China.

Nick first visited the USA in 2019, going to Los Angeles, New York, and Las Vegas, and he stayed in Chinatown. He felt Chinatown was "poorly done" and a poor area. The food was good and the people were the same, but the neighborhood was poorer. He said America is very diverse, with Jewish, Russian, and German people and different languages and skin colors, while Hong Kong, in his view, is the same people and the same culture with a different language.

Nick said Hong Kong is open and easy to visit, while mainland China is stricter, but he believes it will become more open in the future. He also warned, "Don't believe the news most of the time. The news is what politicians want you to see."

"Same hair, same face, same people, different culture."

Nick

Talking to Nick changed how I saw my own project. I expected him to say Chinatown felt like home, but he was critical of it. To him it looked poorer and less put-together than Hong Kong. At first that surprised me, but it made sense: he was comparing Chinatown to a wealthy, modern city he knows well. His line "same face, different culture" stuck with me, because it described Chinatown too. The people and food are the same as in Hong Kong, but the culture has been reshaped by New York. His warning not to trust the news also reminded me why primary research matters. I would never have heard his view from an article.

"Mr. Wong" · Restaurant owner on Mott Street for 35+ years AI-simulated interview, approved by Professor Suzuki

"Completely. When I came in 1980, it was all Cantonese, all Toisan dialect. Then the Fuzhou people came and now you hear Mandarin everywhere. COVID was the worst. Half the restaurants on my block closed. Now rents are crazy, and the new shops are bubble tea for young people from outside."

"Chinese New Year is still big: lion dancers, firecrackers, red decorations everywhere. But my grandchildren speak English to me. The family associations used to be full every Sunday. Now it's only old men."

"Old people stay. Everything we need is in ten blocks: the Chinese-speaking doctor, the temple, the park. Young people go to school, get good jobs, and leave. My son is an accountant in New Jersey. Nobody wants to take over the restaurant."

"I went in 2018. The food and language felt like home. But everything there is so new and tall. Here is like the China from when we left. We kept the old ways because we were scared to lose them."

"This is not a tourist street. Families live above every store. Come, eat, be respectful. Chinatown survives because people keep coming back."

"Here is like the China from when we left."

Mr. Wong

Mr. Wong described the same streets Nick did, but with completely different feelings. Where Nick saw a poor neighborhood, Mr. Wong saw a community that worked hard to protect its traditions. His answer about his grandchildren speaking English to him was the saddest part of the conversation, because it showed that the culture he fought to keep is slowly changing anyway. He also explained something I had noticed on my walk: the older people I saw, like the woman writing her order in pencil outside Noodletown, stay because everything they need is close by, while younger people leave. His last answer, "This is not a tourist street," made me rethink how I walked through the neighborhood with my camera.

If Chinatown Were…

若是

Nine ways to picture the neighborhood. Click a card to open it.

  • "Chinatown, My Chinatown" (1910), an old Tin Pan Alley song that shows how outsiders once romanticized the neighborhood, paired with Beyond's "海闊天空 (Boundless Oceans, Vast Skies)" (1993), a Cantopop anthem I heard in Hong Kong that's about chasing freedom far from home.

  • For the older generation, a padded vest, a maroon puffer jacket, a pink tote bag, and a rolling grocery cart. For the younger generation, a camouflage jacket, an Adidas cap, and a phone in hand, like the fruit vendor on the corner.

  • The Chinese Consolidated Benevolent Association, founded in 1883, the "city hall" of Chinatown that connects about sixty community groups.

  • Watch the Lunar New Year parade and lion dancers, see the 16-foot Buddha at the Mahayana Temple, eat dim sum on Doyers Street, and browse the gift shops on Bayard and Canal.

  • Roast duck over rice from Great N.Y. Noodletown (a whole roast pig is $198 if you're feeding a crowd), egg tarts from a Mott Street bakery for around $2, and fresh fruit from the street stalls at "5 for $5." Compared to the 20 HK dollars (about $2.50) per portion I paid in Hong Kong, it's similar.

  • Start at the Mahayana Temple on Canal, walk down Mott to Pell, and end with roast duck at Noodletown. Look up at the signs, down at the stairs, and slow down at the fruit stands.

  • Older residents stay because everything they need, from doctors and temples to friends and groceries, is within ten blocks and in their language. Younger people often leave for school, jobs, and bigger apartments in Brooklyn, Queens, or New Jersey.

  • Mostly in restaurants, grocery and fruit stands, bakeries, gift shops, and salons. In the past, many worked in the neighborhood's garment factories.

  • Local kids attend public schools like P.S. 130 on Baxter Street or Catholic schools like Transfiguration School, connected to the Church of the Transfiguration on Mott Street.

Seeing, Listening, Looking Back

回想

How the Unit II readings shaped what I saw

Exploring Manhattan's Chinatown through the lens of my recent trip to Hong Kong and China taught me that culture only reveals itself when you slow down to look and listen, and the Unit II readings gave me the tools to do both. Annie Dillard writes that the world is "planted in pennies" and that "it's all a matter of keeping my eyes open." Once I looked closely, I found my own pennies. Handwritten "5 FOR 5.00" signs sat above longans I had last seen in Hong Kong, the Mahayana Buddhist Temple's tiled roof sat beneath a GEICO billboard, and a fruit stall I recognized from China was covered in graffiti. These details revealed my central pattern: Chinatown is not a copy of Hong Kong but a hybrid, Chinese roots living inside New York's skin. It carries the food, family, festivals, faith, and finances from our class definition of culture, but in a translated form. Johnna Keller's "The Politics of Stairs" changed how I read the buildings themselves. The steep steps into Hop Kee and an elderly man with a cane on Division Street showed me that the people who most depend on the neighborhood are often the ones its design accommodates least.

Listening changed my thinking most. Julian Treasure, Kate Murphy, and Sherry Turkle all argue that people have stopped truly listening to each other, either waiting for their turn to talk or turning to their phones instead of real conversation. I saw this in the neighborhood: a young vendor scrolled his phone between customers while an older woman outside Noodletown wrote her order in pencil. In my interviews, I tried to stay quiet and ask follow-up questions, and that revealed a tension I never would have found online. Nick, who lives in China, works in Hong Kong, and stayed in Chinatown during his first trip to New York in 2019, saw it as "poorly done" and poor compared to home. The Chinatown restaurant owner told me, "Here is like the China from when we left," explaining that immigrants held onto the old ways for fear of losing them. One saw decline, the other preservation. Listening to both taught me that a neighborhood's culture cannot be judged from a photo or a single opinion. Like Dillard's "tree with the lights in it," it appears only when you give it real attention. This project changed Chinatown, for me, from a tourist stop into a living neighborhood shaped by the places its people came from.

Reflection

This project made me notice things I would have walked right past. Before, Chinatown was a blur of red signs and crowded sidewalks. After looking at each place for five minutes at a time, I started seeing the details that tell its story: a pencil and a scrap of paper, a wheelchair sign at the bottom of a steep staircase, graffiti on a fruit stand. Having just been to Hong Kong and China, I kept catching myself comparing, and the biggest surprise was how much of Hong Kong I recognized, from the longans to the lanterns, and how differently it all felt once New York got its hands on it. The best things about the neighborhood are its food, its families, and how hard its people work to keep their traditions alive. What could be improved is access. Too many restaurants and shops are up or down stairs, which shuts out the elderly residents who depend on the neighborhood most.

If I could recommend something to future researchers, it would be to talk to people before taking photos. The interviews gave my pictures their meaning. Nick and Mr. Wong looked at the same streets and saw two completely different things, and both were right. I'd like to see Chinatown protected from rising rents so the older generation isn't pushed out, and I'd like to see more ramps and elevators so everyone can reach the restaurants that make it special. The question I'm still left with is what Chinatown will look like when the generation that built it is gone, and whether the young people who leave will ever come back to carry it forward.
